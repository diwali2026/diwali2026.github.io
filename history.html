History & Origins

Diwali’s roots reach into seasonal harvest rites of the Indian subcontinent. Over centuries it absorbed Puranic rituals, courtly patronage, travellers’ descriptions, industrial fireworks, diaspora identity, and contemporary debates about pollution and eco-friendly celebration.

Ancient origins

Indologists often describe Diwali as likely emerging from a fusion of harvest festivals in ancient India—timed near the post-monsoon agricultural cycle and the Kartika new moon. Lighting lamps symbolically linked household wellbeing to cosmic light; later religious literature layered deities, epics, and ethical meanings onto these seasonal practices.

The festival’s longevity is attested not only in Sanskrit religious texts but also in inscriptions and in accounts by foreign visitors who watched cities blaze with oil lamps centuries ago.

Early textual mentions

  • Padma Purana and Skanda Purana (composed roughly between the 7th and 10th centuries CE in scholarly estimates) prescribe lamp-lighting and related Kartika observances. Ritual details vary by text—some stress lamps for Yama; others emphasise Lakshmi and household illumination.
  • The Sanskrit play Nagananda attributed to emperor Harsha (7th century) includes references to a lamp festival (Deepapratipadotsava) in Act IV interludes—an early literary glimpse of festive lighting.
  • Jain literature preserves important early vocabulary: Acharya Jinasena’s Harivamsa Purana uses a related form (dipalikaya) in connection with Mahavira’s nirvana tradition; the Kalpa Sutra recounts gods illuminating the night of Mahavira’s liberation.
  • Secondary historical surveys also note Kashmir’s Nilamata Purana and medieval literary works (e.g., Somadeva’s Yasastilaka) among sources illuminating early medieval practice.

Travellers’ accounts

  • Al-Biruni (11th century), in his memoir on India, recorded Hindus celebrating Deepavali on the new-moon day of Kartika.
  • Niccolò de’ Conti (Venetian merchant, early 15th century) described innumerable oil lamps inside temples and on roofs, kept burning day and night, with families in new clothes singing, dancing, and feasting.
  • Domingo Paes (Portuguese traveller, 16th century) wrote of Dipavali in the Vijayanagara Empire: householders illuminating homes and temples with lamps in October.

Evolution — Before (ancient & medieval)

Ancient / early medieval

Harvest lamps become sacred calendar

Seasonal lighting and Kartika observances crystallise in Puranic ritual manuals. Diwali becomes a multi-day complex: wealth worship, Naraka victory themes, Lakshmi puja, Bali/Govardhan motifs, and sibling rites—as later summarised by scholars such as P.V. Kane in History of the Dharmasastra.

Inscriptions

Public commemorations

Historical surveys cite inscriptions commemorating Dipotsava (e.g., references associated with Rashtrakuta contexts) and Jain merchant inscriptions mentioning oil offerings to Jinendra on Diwali—evidence of both Hindu and Jain public observance.

Courts & cities

Urban spectacle

Travellers’ memoirs show Diwali as a visible urban festival—temples, rooftops, and streets lit for communal celebration—well before modern electricity or mass-produced fireworks.

Evolution — During (Mughal & colonial eras)

Mughal period

Courtly coexistence & illumination

Secondary historical writing notes that Mughal political alliances with Hindu polities helped normalise shared festive cultures; by Akbar’s era, Diwali celebrations are described as part of the broader courtly calendar of illuminations and exchange. Sikh memory of Guru Hargobind’s release (Bandi Chhor Divas) also crystallises in this wider early-modern north Indian context.

Fireworks history

From royal pyrotechnics to popular crackers

Research features in Indian media note that gunpowder knowledge entered South Asia centuries earlier, while fireworks as Diwali mass entertainment expanded especially with industrial manufacture and urban middle-class celebration in the colonial and late colonial periods—transforming a lamp festival’s soundscape.

Colonial modernity

Print, markets, and civic festivals

Diwali increasingly appears in newspapers, bazaar advertising, and municipal life. Diaspora communities under indenture and migration (Caribbean, Africa, Southeast Asia) carried Deepavali abroad, where it later gained official holiday recognition in several countries.

Evolution — After (modern, global, contested)

20th–21st century

National holiday & diaspora landmark

In independent India, Diwali is a major gazetted festive period. Abroad, Deepavali is an official public holiday in multiple countries with large South Asian communities (commonly listed examples include Fiji, Guyana, Malaysia, Mauritius, Myanmar, Nepal, Singapore, Sri Lanka, Suriname, and Trinidad and Tobago—alongside recognition in other jurisdictions). Cities worldwide host public lightings and cultural events.

Environment

Firecracker & pollution debates

Urban air-quality crises—especially in Delhi-NCR—have driven court orders, temporary bans, and promotion of “green crackers,” community laser shows, and diya-centric eco-Diwali campaigns. National Geographic and other outlets note criticism of fireworks’ pollution impact alongside continuing popular demand.

Today

Eco-friendly & digital Diwali

Modern practice mixes tradition and innovation: LED strings beside clay diyas, online gift commerce, virtual family pujas across time zones, and sustainability messaging (less plastic décor, oil lamps, community clean-ups). The festival remains a living tradition—adaptive rather than frozen.
