Historical Facts

A selection of well-sourced facts that illuminate how Diwali grew from lamp rites and harvest time into a global festival of lights.

On this page

  • Notable facts
  • References

Notable facts

  1. The name is literally about lamps. Sanskrit Dīpāvalī means a row or garland of lamps—capturing the festival’s most visible ritual before any single myth.
  2. It is multi-religious. Hindus, Jains, Sikhs, and some Buddhists (including Newar Buddhists) observe related light festivals in the same season with distinct theologies of victory and liberation.
  3. Puranas describe it over a millennium ago. Mentions in the Padma Purana and Skanda Purana (roughly 7th–10th centuries in scholarly dating) show structured Kartika lamp rituals.
  4. Al-Biruni saw it in the 11th century. The Persian polymath recorded Deepavali on Kartika’s new moon—an outsider’s confirmation of the festival’s medieval popularity.
  5. Renaissance-era Europeans described the blaze of lamps. Niccolò de’ Conti (15th c.) and Domingo Paes (16th c.) wrote of temples and rooftops covered in oil lamps during Indian light festivals.
  6. Jain sources preserve early Diwali vocabulary. Forms related to “row of lamps” appear in Jain texts connected to Mahavira’s nirvana tradition; the Kalpa Sutra narrates divine illumination at his liberation.
  7. Harsha’s court drama mentions a lamp festival. Nagananda (7th century) includes Deepapratipadotsava references—literary evidence beyond the Puranas.
  8. Bandi Chhor Divas coincides with Diwali timing. Sikh remembrance of Guru Hargobind’s release intertwines freedom narratives with the Festival of Lights and the illumination of Amritsar’s Harmandir Sahib.
  9. Fireworks are historically layered onto an older lamp festival. Research features note gunpowder’s earlier arrival and the later popularisation of crackers as mass Diwali entertainment—helping explain today’s eco debates.
  10. Diwali is an official holiday in multiple countries. Beyond India and Nepal, Deepavali is recognised as a public holiday in several nations with South Asian diasporas (commonly including places such as Fiji, Guyana, Malaysia, Mauritius, Singapore, Sri Lanka, Suriname, and Trinidad and Tobago).
  11. Business communities treat it as a financial new year. Closing old account books and opening new ones on Diwali remains a living mercantile custom in many Indian trading communities.
  12. 2026’s main Lakshmi Puja falls on 8 November. Aggregated calendar sources (Wikipedia Diwali page; Drik Panchang examples for New Delhi) place the Amavasya evening puja on Sunday, 8 November 2026, with Dhanteras on 6 November.
