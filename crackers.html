🧯 Safety first — tips for kids

  • Always with a grown-up. Never light crackers on your own.
  • Keep a bucket of water (and sand) nearby. Drop used sparklers into the water.
  • Wear fitted cotton clothes — no loose, flowing or synthetic clothes that catch fire easily.
  • Stay far back. Light from arm’s length with a long incense stick (agarbatti), then walk away. Use open spaces, away from homes, cars and wires.
  • Never re-light a dud or hold a cracker in your hand (except sparklers and pencils). Never put crackers in tins or bottles.
  • Protect ears, eyes and pets. Choose light-and-colour crackers over loud bombs.
  • If someone gets burnt: cool the burn under clean running tap water for 15–20 minutes. No ice, toothpaste, ghee, oil or home remedies. Cover with clean cloth and see a doctor for deep burns or burns on the face, hands, feet or joints.

Sources: PESO Do’s & Don’ts; AIIMS Jammu burns-care advice.[10][11]

History of fireworks in India

Gunpowder was invented in China and spread across Eurasia with the Mongols; Turkic armies brought firearms into India.[3] The chronicle Tarikh-i-Firishta (written 1606–07) says an envoy of Hulegu Khan was welcomed to Delhi in 1258 with a display of aatish-baazi (fireworks), and the Tarikh-i-Firuzshahi describes evening fireworks on Shab-i-Barat under Sultan Firoz Shah Tughlaq.[1][3]

By the 1440s, the Persian ambassador Abd al-Razzaq described fireworks, music and dancing at a festival at the Vijayanagara court.[1] The Mughals and their Rajput contemporaries used fireworks widely for weddings, coronations and festivals; Abu’l Fazl’s Ain-i-Akbari records Emperor Akbar honouring Diwali and calling the worship of fire and light “a religious duty and divine praise.”[1][2] A 17th-century painting of Dara Shikoh’s wedding procession shows the night sky lit by fireworks.[2]

Historians note fireworks became closely linked with Diwali from the late 18th century (for example in Lucknow paintings), and that bursting crackers at home on a large scale became common only after Independence, once fireworks became affordable.[1][2]

Sivakasi: India’s fireworks hub

In 1922, two young cousins from Sivakasi in Tamil Nadu — P. Ayya Nadar and A. Shanmuga Nadar — travelled to Calcutta to learn how matches were made. They returned and set up Sivakasi’s first match unit in 1923, then moved on to colour matches and sparklers, and travelled to Calcutta again around 1930 to learn firecracker making.[4][5]

When World War II cut off imported fireworks (1938–44), the local industry grew quickly. The Indian Explosives Rules of 1940 introduced licensing, and big names such as National Fireworks, Kaliswari Fireworks and Standard Fireworks began in 1942. Sivakasi’s low rainfall and dry climate suit year-round production; by 2001 there were about 450 factories in Sivakasi alone.[6] The town is often called “Little Japan.”[7]

How crackers work & where colours come from

A cracker is a tiny controlled fire. It mixes a fuel (something that burns) with an oxidiser (which supplies oxygen so it burns fast), plus special colour-makers. Bright white light comes from burning metals like aluminium or magnesium.[8][9]

Colours come from metal salts. Heat gives the metal’s electrons extra energy; when they settle back down they let it out as light of one particular colour — like each metal having its own favourite crayon.[8]

Strontium → red Calcium → orange Sodium → yellow Barium → green Copper → blue Aluminium / magnesium → white

Barium (green) is among the metals of concern for air pollution, which is why green crackers avoid barium nitrate.[13]

Traditional crackers

Names vary a lot by region and brand; below are common English, Hindi and Tamil names. Press Play demo for a safe on-screen simulation.

Sparkler

Sparkler

Hindi: phuljhadi · Tamil: kambi mathappu

A wire coated with a paste that burns slowly from the tip, throwing tiny golden-white sparks. Often the first “cracker” children hold — always with an adult and at arm’s length.

Flower

Flower pot

Hindi: anar · Tamil: pussvaanam

A small cone placed on the ground that shoots a fountain of sparks upward, like a blooming flower. It makes light, not a loud bang.

Ground

Ground spinner

Hindi: chakri / ghirni · Tamil: bhoo chakram

A coiled tube that spins very fast on the ground, drawing a glowing circle of sparks that often changes colour.

Rocket

Rocket

Hindi: rocket / hawai

Launched from a bottle or tube: a burning charge pushes it skyward, and it bursts high up. Never launch near buildings, wires or trees.

Sound

Sound crackers (sutli bomb, atom bomb, Lakshmi bomb) Loud

Tamil: Lakshmi vedi, atom bomb

Paper- or thread-wrapped crackers made for a loud bang. India limits cracker noise to 125 dB(AI) at 4 metres, and many very loud bombs break these rules — light them only from a long distance, if at all.

Chain

Chain crackers (ladi / 1000-wala) Restricted

Hindi: ladi, bijli · Tamil: saram

Dozens to thousands of small crackers joined on one fuse that pop one after another. The Supreme Court (2018) accepted a ban on joined/series crackers because of their air, noise and litter problems.

Colour

Colour pencil

Hindi/English: pencil

A thin stick that glows with a bright coloured flame — red, green, yellow — with few sparks. A gentle, hand-held favourite.

Snake

Snake tablet Smoky

Hindi: saanp ki goli · Tamil: pambu maathirai

A small black tablet that, when lit, grows into a long, curly grey “snake” of ash. It makes no bang but gives off thick smoke, so stay in the open and don’t breathe it.

Roll

Roll caps & toy pistol

English: caps, roll caps

Tiny dots of charge on a paper strip that make a sharp “tak!” in a toy pistol. Never fire close to anyone’s ears or eyes.

Aerial

Aerial shots

English: sky shot, aerial shot

A tube that fires one or more glowing shells into the sky where they burst into colourful stars — a mini version of big display fireworks.

New & modern types

Multi-shot

Multi-shot cakes / repeaters

English: multi-shot, cake, repeater

Many tubes joined in one box, fired one after another from a single fuse to make a short show of bursts.

Sky

Sky shots (colour bursts)

English: sky shot

Single or 12/30/60-shot boxes that throw coloured stars high in the air; popular as a quieter alternative to bombs.

Colour

Colour smoke

English: colour smoke

Produces clouds of pink, blue or yellow smoke for photos. Fun to look at — but still smoke, so use outdoors only.

LED

LED / electric sparklers Eco

English: LED sparkler

Battery-powered light wands that look like sparklers but have no flame, smoke or burns — a great choice for very young kids.

Drone

Drone light shows Eco

English: drone show

Hundreds of lit drones fly in formation to draw diyas, lamps and pictures in the sky. Ayodhya’s Deepotsav has featured drone shows.

Laser

Laser shows Eco

English: laser show

Coloured beams and patterns projected into the sky or onto buildings — no smoke and no noise, only light.

Green crackers

Since January 2018, CSIR-NEERI (Nagpur) has worked on lower-emission formulas. Its three families are SWAS (Safe Water Releaser — releases water vapour to suppress dust), STAR (Safe Thermite Cracker) and SAFAL (Safe Minimal Aluminium).[12][13] They aim to cut particulate matter by at least 30% compared with conventional crackers, and avoid barium nitrate.[12][13]

How to check: genuine green crackers carry the CSIR-NEERI green logo and a QR code you can scan with the “CSIR NEERI Green QR Code” app to see the certification.[13][14] Words like “eco-friendly” on a box are not proof.[13]

Green crackers are less polluting, not pollution-free — they still release smoke, particles and noise.[13]

Rules & environment

  • Supreme Court, 23 October 2018 (Arjun Gopal v. Union of India): only improved and green crackers may be sold; on Diwali and other festivals, crackers may be burst only from 8 pm to 10 pm (11:55 pm–12:30 am on Christmas and New Year’s Eve). The Court accepted the ban on joined (series/ladi) crackers and called for community fireworks where possible.[15][16] Local governments may set stricter rules — check your city’s order each year.
  • Noise limit: crackers louder than 125 dB(AI) or 145 dB(C)pk at 4 metres are prohibited; limits are lower for each cracker in a joined series.[17]
  • Air quality: the 2018 order also required pollution boards to monitor aluminium, barium and iron in the air for 14 days around Diwali.[15] A study found high barium levels in Delhi’s air after Diwali, which drove the shift away from barium nitrate.[18]
  • Be kind to others: loud crackers can harm hearing and frighten babies, elderly people and animals.[19] Light, colour and drone shows are kinder choices.
