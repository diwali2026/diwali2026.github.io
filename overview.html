Diwali

The Festival of Lights — a celebration of light over darkness, knowledge over ignorance, and good over evil.

Sanskrit: Dīpāvalī 2026 main day: 8 Nov Hindu · Jain · Sikh · Newar Buddhist

Diwali (also spelled Deepavali or Dipavali) is one of the most widely celebrated festivals of the Indian subcontinent and its global diaspora. Observed chiefly by Hindus, and also by Jains, Sikhs, and some Buddhists (notably Newar Buddhists of Nepal), it centres on the lighting of oil lamps (diyas), prayer, feasting, gift-giving, and family gatherings.

Scholars describe the festival as likely a fusion of ancient harvest observances that later acquired layered religious meanings. Its spiritual themes—victory of dharma over adharma, light over darkness—are shared across traditions even when the associated legends differ by region and faith.

Name and meaning

The word Diwali comes from Sanskrit Dīpāvalī (दीपावली), a compound of dīpa (“lamp”) and āvali (“row, series, garland”). It literally means “a row (or garland) of lamps.” In southern India the form Deepavali is common; in Nepal the related festival season is often called Tihar (and Swanti among Newar communities).

What is Diwali?

Diwali falls in the Hindu lunisolar months of Ashvin/Ashwayuja and Kartika—typically between mid-October and mid-November in the Gregorian calendar—around the new moon (amāvasyā). Celebrations generally last five or six days, with the darkest night as a focal point for Lakshmi Puja in many regions.

Common practices include thoroughly cleaning and decorating homes, drawing rangoli (or kolam) patterns, lighting rows of clay lamps, performing puja (especially to Lakshmi and Ganesha), sharing sweets, exchanging gifts, and—where permitted—fireworks. For many business communities, Diwali also marks the start of a financial new year.

“Diwali is India’s most important festival of the year—a time to celebrate the triumph of light over darkness, knowledge over ignorance, and good over evil.” — National Geographic

Dates and the 2026 calendar

Because Hindu festival dates follow lunar tithis, Gregorian dates shift each year. For 2026, widely cited calendars (including Wikipedia’s Diwali page and Indian panchang sources such as Drik Panchang) place the main Diwali / Lakshmi Puja on Sunday, 8 November 2026. A typical northern five-day sequence for 2026 is:

  • 6 Nov (Fri) — Dhanteras
  • 7 Nov (Sat) — Naraka Chaturdashi / Choti Diwali (Kali Chaudas in some regions)
  • 8 Nov (Sun) — Lakshmi Puja / main Diwali (Amavasya evening)
  • 9–10 Nov — Govardhan Puja / Annakut / Bali Pratipada (exact civil date can vary by local panchang)
  • 10–11 Nov — Bhai Dooj

Note: In some years (including aspects of 2026 calendars), morning and evening rituals associated with different tithis can fall on the same Gregorian date. Always check a local panchang for muhurat timings.

The five days

Day 1

Dhanteras

Auspicious for prosperity. Devotees honour Lakshmi, Kubera, and Dhanvantari; many buy gold, silver, or utensils and light a Yama Deepam.

Day 2

Naraka Chaturdashi

Also called Choti Diwali. Linked to Krishna’s defeat of Narakasura; often includes early oil baths (abhyanga snan) and lighting lamps.

Day 3

Lakshmi Puja

The main night. Families worship Lakshmi and Ganesha, illuminate homes with diyas, share feasts, and in many places set off fireworks.

Day 4

Govardhan Puja

Also Annakut / Bali Pratipada. Commemorates Krishna lifting Govardhan Hill; large food offerings; marks New Year in some communities (e.g., Gujarati).

Day 5

Bhai Dooj

Celebrates the bond of siblings (Yama–Yamuna legend). Sisters apply tilak and pray for brothers’ wellbeing; gifts are exchanged.

Regional variations

Diwali is not a single uniform ritual. In north India, the Rama–Ayodhya homecoming story is especially prominent. In much of south India, Krishna’s victory over Narakasura is emphasised, and Deepavali morning rituals can differ. In western India, themes of Vishnu and King Bali / Bali Pratipada are strong. In eastern India (notably West Bengal), Kali Puja often coincides with Diwali Amavasya. In Nepal, Tihar unfolds as a multi-day honouring of crows, dogs, cows, Lakshmi, and siblings (Bhai Tika), with Newar Mha Puja marking a new year for many.

Celebrations across religions

Hinduism

Multiple legends converge: Rama’s return to Ayodhya; Krishna and Narakasura; Lakshmi’s association with prosperity (including links to the churning of the ocean in broader Hindu mythology); and seasonal harvest thanksgiving.

Jainism

Jains observe Diwali as the anniversary of Mahavira’s nirvana (liberation) at Pawapuri. The Kalpa Sutra tradition describes gods illuminating the darkness; devotees light lamps to keep knowledge alive. Jain observance often stresses austerity, ahimsa, and avoiding harm—including restraint with fireworks.

Sikhism — Bandi Chhor Divas

Sikhs commemorate Bandi Chhor Divas (“Day of Liberation”), recalling Guru Hargobind’s release from Gwalior Fort and return to Amritsar, traditionally associated with Diwali timing. Gurdwaras, including the Harmandir Sahib (Golden Temple), are illuminated.

Newar Buddhist / Nepalese traditions

Newar Buddhists and broader Nepalese communities observe related light festivals within Tihar/Swanti. Some Buddhist narratives also associate the season with Emperor Ashoka’s turn toward Buddhism (Ashok Vijayadashami themes appear in secondary educational summaries).
