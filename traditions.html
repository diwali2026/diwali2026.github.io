Traditions & Customs

From clay lamps and floor art to sweets and sibling blessings, Diwali customs turn homes into stages of welcome, gratitude, and renewal.

Cleaning and preparation

In the days before Diwali, families traditionally deep-clean homes and workplaces. The practice is practical (post-monsoon refresh) and symbolic: a clean, welcoming space for Lakshmi. Doors and thresholds may be decorated with mango leaves, marigold garlands, and footprints drawn to “invite” the goddess inside.

Diyas and lights

Diyas (earthen oil lamps) are the festival’s emblem. Filled with oil and a cotton wick, they are placed in rows on windowsills, courtyards, rooftops, and riverbanks. Electric string lights are now common, but many households still keep at least some clay lamps for ritual authenticity. Light signifies knowledge, hope, and the driving back of spiritual darkness.

Rangoli, kolam, and floor art

Rangoli (also known regionally as kolam, muggu, alpana, etc.) are decorative patterns made with coloured powders, rice flour, sand, or flower petals—often at entrances. Designs range from geometric lattices to peacocks, lotuses, and diya motifs. They beautify the threshold and are popularly said to welcome guests and good fortune.

Puja and prayer

On the main night, many Hindu families perform Lakshmi–Ganesha puja during the evening pradosh period. Offerings may include flowers, sweets, coins, account books (for businesses), and lamps. Temples hold special aarti; some regions emphasise Kali Puja on the same Amavasya. Jain communities gather for hymns and remembrance of Mahavira; Sikh gurdwaras illuminate and mark Bandi Chhor Divas with prayer and community meals (langar).

Sweets and festive food

Diwali is inseparable from mithai: laddoos, barfi, jalebi, gulab jamun, kaju katli, chakli, and countless regional specialities. Families exchange sweet boxes with neighbours and colleagues. Savoury snacks, elaborate vegetarian feasts, and—on Govardhan Puja/Annakut—mountain-like arrays of food offerings appear in Vaishnava temples and homes. Sharing food expresses prosperity meant to be circulated, not hoarded.

Fireworks

Sparklers, flowerpots, and aerial fireworks became a defining (and debated) sound of urban Diwali in the modern era. Some cities restrict timing or ban certain crackers for air quality and safety. Eco-conscious celebrations emphasise lamps, community events, and quieter alternatives. Jain tradition, guided by ahimsa, often discourages fireworks that harm living beings.

Gifts, clothes, and charity

Wearing new clothes is a widespread custom. Gifts—sweets, dry fruits, silver coins, utensils on Dhanteras—circulate among relatives and business associates. Many also practise dāna (charity): donations to temples, food banks, or neighbours in need, aligning celebration with generosity.

The gambling tradition

A long-attested folk custom links Diwali nights with recreational gambling (cards, dice) in some communities, framed by legends that Goddess Parvati played dice with Shiva, or that try-your-luck games invite prosperity. Practices vary widely; many families omit gambling entirely. Historical ritual summaries (e.g., Kane’s overview of five principal ritual themes) list dice-play among traditional associations of the festival complex.

North

Rama narrative, Lakshmi Puja, Bhai Dooj, large public lightings (e.g., Ayodhya Deepotsav in contemporary times).

South

Narakasura victory emphasis, early oil baths, Deepavali morning sweets, kolam artistry.

West

Bali Pratipada / New Year themes (e.g., Gujarati Bestu Varas), Annakut offerings.

East & Himalaya

Kali Puja (Bengal); Tihar animal-honouring days and Bhai Tika (Nepal).
