Ramayana & Other Legends

Diwali has no single origin myth. Across India and neighbouring regions, different communities remember different victories of light—yet they share a moral horizon of homecoming, liberation, and renewed order.

The Ramayana story and Diwali

In much of northern India, Diwali commemorates the return of Rama, Sita, and Lakshmana to Ayodhya after fourteen years of exile and after the defeat of the demon-king Ravana. According to tradition, the people of Ayodhya lit rows of oil lamps to welcome them home on a moonless night—an image that still shapes how millions understand the “festival of lights.”

Rama, an incarnation of Vishnu in Vaishnava theology, embodies ideal kingship (maryādā puruṣottama). Sita—often understood as an incarnation of Lakshmi—has been abducted by Ravana and rescued with the aid of allies including Hanuman and the vanara forces. The homecoming is thus both a family reunion and a restoration of righteous rule (Ram Rajya).

Diwali, in this telling, is fundamentally a homecoming story: light offered in welcome and in gratitude that justice has prevailed. — paraphrasing themes discussed by the Rubin Museum and National Geographic

Key figures

  • Rama — prince of Ayodhya; exile, war, and return.
  • Sita — Rama’s consort; her rescue is central to the epic’s climax.
  • Lakshmana — Rama’s loyal brother who shares the exile.
  • Ravana — king of Lanka; defeated at the end of the war.
  • Hanuman — devoted ally whose leap to Lanka and service become models of bhakti and courage.

Link to Dussehra (Vijayadashami)

Dussehra (Vijayadashami), which falls about twenty days before Diwali in the lunar calendar sequence of many regions, celebrates Rama’s victory over Ravana (and, in other regional frames, Durga’s victory). Effigies of Ravana are burned in many north Indian cities. Diwali then marks the subsequent joyous return to Ayodhya. The two festivals form a narrative arc: victory, then homecoming and illumination.

Krishna and Narakasura

In large parts of south India and in other regional tellings, Diwali (especially Naraka Chaturdashi) commemorates Krishna’s defeat of the demon Narakasura, who had imprisoned thousands and terrorised his realm. The victory is celebrated with early-morning baths, oil lamps, and sweets—another “light over darkness” narrative centred on Vishnu’s avatar Krishna rather than Rama.

Lakshmi and the churning of the ocean

Diwali is also closely associated with Lakshmi, goddess of prosperity and fortune. Broader Hindu mythology recounts Lakshmi’s emergence during the Samudra Manthan (churning of the ocean of milk). While that myth is not unique to Diwali night alone, Lakshmi Puja on Diwali Amavasya became a central household rite: families clean homes, draw rangoli, light lamps at entrances, and invite prosperity for the year ahead. In agrarian history, the festival’s timing near harvest’s end reinforced prayers for wealth and security through winter.

Mahavira’s nirvana (Jain tradition)

For Jains, Diwali marks the night of Mahavira’s nirvana—final liberation—at Pawapuri. Tradition holds that the light of the gods departed with him; kings and communities lit earthen lamps so that ordinary light would continue where spiritual light had ascended. The Kalpa Sutra preserves this imagery. Jain Diwali emphasises knowledge, restraint, and non-violence.

Guru Hargobind and Bandi Chhor Divas

Sikhs observe Bandi Chhor Divas, remembering the release of the sixth Guru, Guru Hargobind, from Gwalior Fort (traditionally dated to the early 17th century under Jahangir). Narratives recount that he secured the freedom of fifty-two princes as well—often told through the motif of a cloak with fifty-two tassels. His return toward Amritsar is associated with Diwali-time illuminations of the Harmandir Sahib. The day celebrates freedom and justice alongside the wider Festival of Lights.
